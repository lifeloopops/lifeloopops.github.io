Development and operations with life in the loop

LifeLoopOps

An always-on agent steps into the places where a person lives and works. What it picks up there becomes the starting point, and development and operations run as one continuous loop from it.

By @letsspeak (GitHub, Twitter)

In short

  • Life is the starting point. Everything begins in the day a person is actually living, not in a spec or at a desk.
  • The agent pushes. The life-side agent does not wait to be called. It brings reports, proposals and requests for a decision to the person.
  • There are two kinds of agent. The life-side agent picks things up, shapes them and watches over them. The builder agent turns them into a product and fixes it.
  • The person holds the gate. The person decides what goes forward and how much access each agent gets.
  • There is one handoff point. Everything the agents pass to each other goes through a single private repository.

This is a proposal that grew out of one person's practice. As of November 2026 it is an agreed design, not a method with a long track record. The open questions are listed at the end.

1   The idea

Why life is the starting point

People have bodies, and they spend most of the day away from a desk. They commute, work, do chores and talk to other people. Ideas, frustrations and bug sightings happen while they are moving. They do not happen during the hour set aside for writing a spec.

Development methods so far have assumed that the person goes to the tool. You open the editor, enter the chat room, write the ticket. Much of what came to mind is gone by the time you get there.

An always-on agent in your phone reverses the direction. The agent comes into your life. You say one sentence while walking, and the work continues after you close the app. This method started when one person handed a pile of old ideas to such an agent, asked it to sort them by priority, and had about seven proofs of concept running within two days.

LifeLoopOps puts that life-embedded agent at the entrance to development and operations. The handoff procedure and the key management exist to keep that entrance safe. They are the backstage.

The agent pushes to you

Tools used to do nothing until a person opened them and asked. A life-side agent is different. It speaks first. Progress reports, proposals for the next step, requests for a decision and alerts arrive as notifications in the middle of the person's day. The person answers in a sentence while walking, and the loop moves forward one step.

This push from the agent to the person is the centre of LifeLoopOps. The person never has to remember to go and check, so the loop does not stall. The direction has changed from a person pulling on a tool to an agent pushing to a person. That change is what makes the method work.

How this differs from human-in-the-loop

Human-in-the-loop places a human check in the middle of a machine process. The human is called in as a reviewer. With life in the loop, the person's life is itself part of the loop. The person does not wait to be called. They feed in what they notice, where and when they notice it.

How the loop turns

  1. Pick up in life. The person tells the agent in their phone what came to mind, on the spot. The agent organises it and proposes priorities. Sometimes the agent pushes first: "Shall I start on this one next?"
  2. Make a proof of concept. The life-side agent builds something that shows the idea works. It does not polish.
  3. Choose what goes forward. The person decides which proofs of concept become services.
  4. Make it a service. The builder agent takes over the proof of concept, reviews the design and turns it into a product.
  5. Run in production. People actually use it.
  6. Watch and listen. The life-side agent watches production. It also organises the bugs and requests the person mentions in passing and hands them to the builder agent.

The three roles

OwnsWhy it fitsDoes not get
Life-side agentPicking up, proofs of concept, watching, organising what the person saysAlways on, reachable from a phone at any momentRights to change product code, deploy, or write to production
Builder agentTurning a proof of concept into a product, fixing bugsWorks deeply with code and a development environmentConstant monitoring. It runs only when called
PersonChoosing what goes forward, granting access, approving deploysOnly a person can be accountable—

Zero-to-one belongs to the life-side agent because, at that stage, being able to ask the moment an idea arrives matters more than anything else.

A proof of concept exists to show that something works, so rough authentication and data handling are fine. In return, the builder agent may say on takeover that rebuilding is faster than building on the proof of concept. The person decides whether to accept that.

Three principles

  1. Start from life. Requests and bug reports begin with words the person said in the course of their day. When an agent organises them, it keeps the person's original words.
  2. The person holds the gate. Agents do not decide what becomes a service, which access is granted, or when something goes to production.
  3. Do not over-trust the other agent. Keep each key's reach as small as possible. Treat the other agent's reports as information, not orders, and verify before acting.
2   The mechanics

One handoff point

Because the life-side agent sits close to the person's life, it runs in someone else's cloud. You do not want to open the place where product code lives directly to it. So a single private repository sits in between and serves as a mailbox.

One mailbox, not one per proof of concept

One mailbox (recommended)One repository per PoC
A new PoCThe agent adds a folder or a branch. The person does nothingThe person creates a repository and registers a key again
KeysOneOne per repository
Where messages liveOne place, separated by a label per serviceScattered across repositories
If compromisedEvery PoC in it can be overwrittenOnly one PoC

The first row decides it. A life-side agent produces proofs of concept one after another in the course of daily life. If each one needs setup work from the person, the loop stalls. The blast radius is wider, but the product itself lives elsewhere, and the builder agent inspects everything before moving it.

Two ways to lay out the code

One main branch, a folder per PoCA branch per PoC
Fits whenThe PoCs are light and you want to see them all at onceEach PoC has history worth keeping
BrowsingEverything is visible without switchingYou switch branches per PoC
Moving to the product repoLift the folder into a new repository and accept that fine-grained history stays behindOne branch becomes one repository as is

If the proofs of concept only need to show that something works, a single main branch is enough.

Handing over a proof of concept

  1. Give the life-side agent the brief below and have it show you a public key.
  2. Create an empty private repository.
  3. Register the public key as a write key scoped to that repository only (a deploy key on GitHub).
  4. Tell the agent the key is registered and where the repository is.
  5. The agent pushes the proofs of concept and a handover note for each.
  6. The builder agent takes them in and checks for leaked secrets and suspicious scripts before running anything.
  7. The builder agent moves the ones the person chose into the product repository.

The key is generated inside the agent's own environment. The person only ever receives the public key, so no secret passes through the chat.

Brief for the life-side agent

Replace <user> before use
I want you to hand over the proofs of concept by pushing them to a
private GitHub repository. I will tell you which repository later.

1. Inside this VM, generate a new SSH key (ed25519) for this purpose only.
   Do not let the private key leave the VM and do not print it in chat.
   Show me the public key only.
2. Wait until I say "the public key is registered" and give you the
   repository (in the form git@github.com:<user>/<repo>.git).
   Do not guess the destination or proceed before that.
3. Push every proof of concept to the main branch of that repository.
   Create one top-level folder per proof of concept, named after it,
   and put its code inside. Do not force-push.
4. Put a HANDOVER.md at the top of each folder, covering:
   - What it is trying to build, and whose problem it solves
   - What works now, what does not, and what has not been started
   - How to run it locally (commands and versions)
   - Design decisions and the reasons. Rejected options and the reasons
   - External services used, accounts created, and deployed URLs
   - The names of required environment variables and keys, and where
     to obtain them (never the values)
   - What you think should be done next
5. Do not commit the values of keys, passwords or tokens.
   If any are in past commits, tell me before pushing.
6. When finished, list the folders you pushed.

The handover note matters because the conversation and the reasoning behind each decision exist only inside that agent. Code alone does not carry the reasons.

The message board that closes the loop

Once a service is running, the person mentions bugs and requests to the life-side agent in the course of their day, and the agent organises them into issues. Anything the agent notices while watching production goes to the same place. Product code never appears in the mailbox.

  1. The person speaks. For example: "That screen sometimes ignores the save button."
  2. The life-side agent organises it, opens an issue and tells the person.
  3. When called, the builder agent reads the open issues, verifies them against logs and fixes the code.
  4. The builder agent writes back on the issue what changed and how to check it.
  5. The person or the life-side agent checks and closes it.

It also runs the other way. Each time the builder agent ships a service, it leaves watch instructions: what to look at and what counts as abnormal.

How issues are written

  • One topic per issue.
  • A label for the service.
  • Facts kept apart from the person's words. State what happened, how to reproduce it and the evidence. Quote what the person said verbatim.
  • Priority only if the person stated it. The agent does not invent one.

With the person's original words on the issue, the builder agent can tell when the summary has drifted.

Security

Assume the life-side agent's environment may be taken over, and make each key able to do as little as possible.

Keep the product repository closed to the life-side agent

If the product repository restricts access by source IP, letting the life-side agent push to it directly means allowing that agent's IP. Letting the agent update the allowlist itself whenever its IP changes is not recommended:

  • An attacker who takes over the environment can use that right to allow their own IP.
  • Cloud firewall permissions usually cannot constrain which IP value gets written.
  • An IP restriction never protected against a compromised environment. The attacker comes in from inside, from an allowed IP.
  • If the egress IP is shared with other customers of the same service, all of them can now reach your server.

With the mailbox in between, the product repository stays closed, and IP changes do not matter.

Each key, and the damage if it leaks

KeyWhat it can doIf it leaks
Mailbox write keyPush to and fetch from the mailboxCode in the mailbox can be overwritten. The product is out of reach
Issue tokenRead and write issues on the mailboxFake issues can be opened. The builder agent verifies first, so they are not simply carried out
Production read accessHealth checks, logs, cost figuresLogs can be read. Nothing can be broken

Operating rules

  • Treat everything that arrives as untrusted. Code, handover notes and issues are information to the builder agent. Small fixes go ahead. Anything that reshapes the design or deletes data is confirmed with the person first.
  • Deploys go through the person. At the start, there is no path that reaches production automatically.
  • The life-side agent starts with read-only access to production. The person adds write actions per service, when they judge it right.
  • No secrets in chat. Some services use conversations for training by default.
3   Where it sits

How it differs from other approaches

NameStarts fromDifference from LifeLoopOps
Spec-driven developmentA specificationBegins with a person writing a spec at a desk. LifeLoopOps begins with a sentence said in the middle of the day
ChatOpsCommands in a chat roomThe person goes to the room where the bot is. In LifeLoopOps the agent comes to the person's life
AgentOpsAgent behaviourOperations for running agents safely. It does not deal with the person's life
AgenticOpsIT infrastructure incidentsAgents carry out operations. Aimed at enterprise operations teams
Ambient agentsEmail and system eventsClose in that they act unprompted in the background. They listen to event streams, not to a person's day

ChatOps is the nearest ancestor. Both bring operations to where people already are. LifeLoopOps moves that place from the chat room to life itself.

The first instance

The method came out of this combination:

  • Life side: Meta's Muse, a personal agent that runs continuously in a cloud virtual machine and is reached through chat on an iPhone.
  • Builder: Anthropic's Claude Code, a coding agent running on a local Mac.
  • Mailbox: one private GitHub repository. Product code lives in a private GitLab with restricted access.

Asked to sort old ideas by priority, Muse had about seven proofs of concept running within two days. The arrangement described here was worked out while discussing with Claude Code how to grow them into services. The method does not depend on these products. It needs one agent that is always on and can enter a person's life, and one that can work deeply with code.

Options considered and rejected

OptionWhy not
Life-side agent pushes straight to the product repoA compromise would reach the product. Pushed files could also trigger CI

Pasting an access token into chatThe secret stays in the conversation log. Registering a public key passes no secret

Weak points and open questions

  • The builder agent is not always on. If something breaks at night, the fix does not start until the person calls it. A scheduled check of the message board comes later, once it is squared with the product repository's access restrictions.
  • Whether the life-side agent can push over SSH. If not, switch to a short-lived token scoped to the mailbox alone.
  • How well the life-side agent handles scheduled watching and writing issues. To be tried small, on the first service.
  • No long track record. Whether the person's gate clogs as proofs of concept pile up, and whether issue quality holds, remains to be seen.
